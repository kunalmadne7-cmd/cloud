# Dr Lal PathLabs buys Thyrocare: a hypothetical deal, explained from the basics

> **This deal has not happened.** It is a thought experiment: Dr Lal PathLabs (DLPL) buys
> API Holdings' (PharmEasy's) controlling stake in Thyrocare, then makes the SEBI open offer that
> the law requires.
>
> **Where the numbers come from.** The company websites, BSE, NSE and SEBI were all blocked from
> the machine this was written on (see `DOWNLOAD_CHECKLIST.md`). So every figure below comes from
> press and aggregator reports *of* the companies' filings, found by web search, and not from the
> filings themselves. Treat them as good first drafts, not audited facts. Anything I worked out
> myself is marked **(derived)**. Anything that needs checking against the annual report is marked
> **(verify)**.
>
> All money is in **₹ crore** (1 crore = 10 million) and **consolidated** unless stated.
> Share prices are as of late September 2026.

## Part 1: The basics

### What does a diagnostics company actually do?

You give a blood sample. A machine in a lab tests it. You get a report. The lab charges a fee
per test. That fee is the whole business.

The costs are:

- **Reagents**: the chemicals the machines use up on every test. This is the "raw material".
- **People**: phlebotomists (the people who take blood), lab technicians, pathologists who sign reports.
- **Collection network**: rent for collection centres and the cost of moving samples to the lab,
  often overnight, often by air.
- **Machines**: expensive analysers that wear out. This shows up as depreciation.

The core trick is **volume**. A lab machine costs the same whether it runs 1,000 tests a night or
100,000. More tests through the same machines means a lower cost per test. So everything in this
industry is about getting samples into your lab.

### The two companies in one line each

**Dr Lal PathLabs** is the trusted consumer brand. Patients, or their doctors, pick DLPL by name.
It is strongest in North India and runs its own network of 7,727 patient service centres and 13,935
pick-up points (FY26).

**Thyrocare** is the low-cost wholesale factory. It rarely deals with the patient directly.
About 10,800 franchisees (small collection centres, clinics, hospitals, apps like PharmEasy) collect
samples and ship them to Thyrocare's giant automated central lab in Navi Mumbai or one of about 32
regional labs. Thyrocare charges the franchisee a wholesale price, and the franchisee adds its own
margin before billing the patient.

**The analogy:** DLPL is a restaurant chain with its own outlets and a name people trust.
Thyrocare is the central kitchen that cooks for 10,000 small eateries, each of which serves the
food under its own name. Two very different ways to make money from the same meal.

### The unit economics tell the story

| | DLPL | Thyrocare |
|---|---|---|
| Revenue per patient | about ₹973 (Q1 FY27) **(derived)** | about ₹432 (FY26) **(derived)** |
| Volume | 8.2 million patients, 25.9 million samples in one quarter (Q1 FY27) | 19.2 million patients, 210 million tests in a year (FY26) |
| Where the patient comes from | Walks in, home collection, doctor referral | Through a franchisee or partner |

Thyrocare earns less than half as much per patient, because it charges the wholesale price and
the franchisee keeps the retail markup. Yet its profit margin is *higher* than DLPL's (see
below), because its costs per test are so low. That is what a factory-scale, fully automated lab
buys you.

*Careful with the comparison:* DLPL counts "samples" and Thyrocare counts "tests". One sample can
carry many tests. Revenue per patient is the fairer comparison, and even that is loose, because a
Thyrocare "patient" is billed at wholesale.

### How ₹100 of revenue turns into profit (FY26)

| Step | DLPL | Thyrocare |
|---|---|---|
| Customer pays | ₹100 | ₹100 |
| Reagents, staff, rent, logistics | ₹72.8 | ₹68.4 |
| **EBITDA** (profit before depreciation, interest and tax) | **₹27.2** | **₹31.6** |
| Depreciation, interest, tax | ₹8.8 | ₹12.0 |
| **Final profit (PAT)** | **₹18.5** | **₹19.6** |

*(derived from FY26 revenue, EBITDA and PAT: DLPL ₹2,763 / ₹752 / ₹510 crore; Thyrocare ₹829 /
₹262 / ₹163 crore.)* The breakdown of the ₹72.8 and ₹68.4 into reagents, staff and other costs
needs the annual report P&L **(verify)**.

**What this tells you:** both are high-margin, asset-light businesses that keep about ₹18–20 of
every ₹100 as final profit. That is why the stock market prices them at 50–60 times earnings.
Thyrocare has the fatter EBITDA margin but spends more of it on depreciation, because it owns
heavy automation.

## Part 3: The deal, step by step

### Step 1: DLPL signs a share purchase agreement (SPA) with API Holdings

DLPL agrees to buy API's **8.12 crore shares (51%)** at a negotiated price. This is a private deal
between two parties, done "off-market".

### Step 2: The law forces an open offer

India's takeover rules (**SEBI SAST Regulations, 2011**) say:

- **Regulation 3:** if you acquire **25% or more** of a listed company's votes, and
- **Regulation 4:** if you acquire **control**,

then you must offer to buy **at least another 26%** from ordinary public shareholders.

**Why this rule exists:** when control of a company changes hands, small shareholders did not get
a say. They bought into a company run by one owner and now it will be run by someone else. The
open offer gives them a fair chance to leave at the same kind of price the big seller got.

**Who the offer is made to:** everyone except the buyer, the seller and the people acting with
them ("persons acting in concert", PACs).

### Step 3: What price must DLPL offer?

**Regulation 8(2)** says that for a frequently traded share like Thyrocare, the offer price must be
**at least the highest of**:

1. the **negotiated price** DLPL pays API Holdings;
2. the volume-weighted average price DLPL (or its PACs) paid for any Thyrocare shares in the
   **52 weeks** before the announcement;
3. the **highest** price DLPL paid in the **26 weeks** before;
4. the **60-trading-day volume-weighted average market price** before the announcement.

In plain words: **the small shareholder gets at least what the big seller gets, and at least the
recent market average.** DLPL has bought no Thyrocare shares, so in practice it comes down to (1)
versus (4).

Anchors that already exist:

- API's own cost: **₹433** a share (2021 price, adjusted for the bonus).
- The August 2026 block sale: **~₹625** average.
- Current price: **~₹549**. 52-week range: ₹343–₹662.

A seller that is no longer desperate and just sold 10% at ~₹625 is unlikely to take less for
*control*. So this primer uses **₹625** as the central case. (That is a +14% premium to today's
price.)

### Step 4: How much money?

| Offer price | Premium to ₹549 | Buy API's 51% | Open offer (26%, if everyone tenders) | **Maximum total** | Implied value of 100% |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| ₹550 | 0% | 4,466 | 2,275 | **6,741** | 8,751 |
| **₹625** | **14%** | **5,075** | **2,585** | **7,660** | **9,944** |
| ₹700 | 28% | 5,684 | 2,896 | **8,580** | 11,138 |

*(derived: 8.12 crore shares for the stake; 26% × ~15.91 crore = ~4.14 crore shares for the offer.)*

At ₹625 DLPL pays about **56x Thyrocare's last 12 months of profit** and about **35x its EBITDA**
**(derived)**. That is not cheap, but it is *less* than DLPL's own ~60x. Part 4 explains why that
detail matters.

**Escrow (Regulation 17):** before the offer opens, DLPL must lock money away to prove it can
pay. The rule is 25% of the first ₹500 crore of offer value plus 10% of the rest. At ₹625 that
is about **₹334 crore** **(derived)**, held as cash, a bank guarantee or securities.

### Step 5: The 75% ceiling

Indian listed companies must keep **at least 25% in public hands** (the *minimum public
shareholding* rule). So promoters can own at most 75%.

51.03% + 26% = **77.03%**. If *every* offered share were tendered, DLPL would be about 2% over the
limit. The law then gives the buyer **12 months to sell down** to 75%, and bars it from
delisting the company in that window.

In reality, this is unlikely to bite (see Step 7). If DLPL wanted to own **all** of Thyrocare and
delist it, SEBI's newer **Regulation 5A** lets a buyer say up front that the open offer is also a
delisting offer. That needs a much higher price, because delisting runs on a reverse book-building
process in which shareholders effectively set the price.

### Step 6: Competition Commission (CCI) approval

Since September 2024, India requires CCI approval for any deal worth **over ₹2,000 crore** where
the target has substantial business in India, even if the target is small by assets or turnover.
At ~₹5,000–7,700 crore this deal is well above that, so **the stake purchase cannot close until
CCI clears it.**

Will CCI object? Probably not on market share. Indian diagnostics is highly fragmented and most
tests are still done by small local labs. But CCI would look closely at one issue: Thyrocare
supplies thousands of small labs and clinics that *compete* with DLPL. Could DLPL squeeze them
through Thyrocare's wholesale prices? That question alone could slow the review.

### Step 7: Will shareholders actually sell in the open offer?

**This is the most interesting practical point, and 2021 already answered it.**

An open offer is just a standing offer to buy. If the market price during the tender window is
*above* the offer price, nobody sensible tenders: they sell on the exchange for more. In 2021 the
offer was ₹1,300 while the market was ₹1,488 at announcement, and only 5% of a possible 26% was
tendered.

So in our deal:

- If DLPL offers ₹625 and the market believes DLPL will run Thyrocare *better*, the share may
  trade **above** ₹625. Acceptance would be low, and DLPL would keep most of its ₹2,585 crore.
- If the market thinks the deal is bad for Thyrocare's minority holders (for example, it fears
  franchisees will leave), the share will trade **below** ₹625. Acceptance would be high and DLPL
  would need the full amount.

DLPL has to **fund the worst case** (full acceptance) but can **hope for the 2021 outcome.**

### Step 8: The timeline

| Day | Event |
|---|---|
| Day 0 | SPA signed. **Public announcement** to the exchanges the same day. |
| Within 5 working days | **Detailed public statement** in newspapers. Escrow funded at least 2 working days before this. |
| Within 5 working days of that | **Draft letter of offer** filed with SEBI. (This is the kind of document the 2021 Thyrocare DLOF is.) |
| ~3 weeks later | SEBI comments. Final **letter of offer** sent to shareholders. |
| 10 working days | **Tendering period**: shareholders tender through their brokers. |
| Within 10 working days of close | Payment to those whose shares were accepted. |
| In parallel | **CCI approval.** The SPA can't close until CCI clears it. |

Realistically **3–6 months**, depending mostly on CCI.

## Part 4: Can DLPL afford it, and does it make DLPL's shareholders richer?

### The funding gap

| | ₹ crore |
|---|---|
| Buy API's 51% at ₹625 | 5,075 |
| Open offer if fully accepted | 2,585 |
| **Worst case total** | **7,660** |
| DLPL cash (June 2026) | 1,693 |
| **Gap** | **~6,000** |

DLPL has always been a net-cash company. It cannot pay for this from its own cash. It has three
ways to pay:

1. **Borrow.**
2. **Pay in DLPL shares** (issue new shares to API Holdings instead of cash).
3. **A mix.**

### The key test: EPS accretion or dilution

"Accretive" means DLPL's **earnings per share (EPS)** goes *up* after the deal. "Dilutive" means
it goes *down*. Here is a simple way to think about it.

When you buy a company at 56x earnings, each ₹100 you spend buys ₹1.77 of yearly profit. That is
Thyrocare's **earnings yield**. Now compare it with what each ₹100 of funding costs DLPL:

| Source of money | Yearly cost to DLPL per ₹100, after tax | vs ₹1.77 that Thyrocare earns |
|---|---|---|
| Borrowing at ~8.5% | ~₹6.36 | Much more expensive → **dilutive** |
| Using its own cash (which earns ~7%) | ~₹5.24 of interest income given up | More expensive → **dilutive** |
| Issuing DLPL shares at 60x | ~₹1.67 (the profit new shareholders now share) | Slightly cheaper → **mildly accretive** |

**Illustration**: buying only API's 51% at ₹625 (the 2021-style case where few shareholders
tender). All figures **(derived)** with assumptions stated. It ignores synergies and the extra
amortisation charge that acquisition accounting would add.

| | Cash + debt (₹1,500 cash, ₹3,575 debt) | All in DLPL shares |
|---|---|---|
| Profit gained (51% of Thyrocare's ~₹176 cr) | +90 | +90 |
| Interest lost on cash, after tax | −79 | 0 |
| Interest paid on new debt, after tax | −227 | 0 |
| New DLPL shares issued | 0 | ~2.6 crore (+15%) |
| **DLPL EPS vs today's ~₹32.5** | **~₹19.7 (−40%)** | **~₹32.8 (+1%)** |
| Lal family stake | unchanged ~55% | falls to ~48% |
| API Holdings ends up with | ₹5,075 cr cash | ~13% of DLPL |

**What this means in plain words:**

- **A cash-and-debt deal would cut DLPL's EPS by about 40% on day one.** Thyrocare's profit is far
  too small to cover the interest. DLPL would need huge synergies or many years of Thyrocare
  growing ~20% a year to break even. A debt-funded deal is hard to justify to DLPL shareholders.
- **A share-swap roughly breaks even on day one,** because DLPL's own shares are priced even
  more richly than Thyrocare's. But it costs the **Lal family its majority**, and it makes
  **PharmEasy's parent a ~13% shareholder in DLPL**, a competitor-turned-part-owner. Both are big
  governance questions.
- The realistic answer is a **mix**: some shares to API (which API may like, since it gets a stake
  in a larger, steadier company instead of a lump of cash), some cash, a little debt.
  A share issue to API would be a **preferential allotment** under SEBI's ICDR rules. It needs
  DLPL shareholder approval, a minimum price set by a formula, and a lock-in.

## Part 5: Why would DLPL want Thyrocare, and what could go wrong?

### The case for

1. **Geography.** DLPL is dominant in the North and has said it is hunting for acquisitions in
   **South India** to fill gaps. Thyrocare is pan-India, with regional labs in Chennai,
   Coimbatore, Bengaluru, Hyderabad, Kolkata and elsewhere.
2. **A second channel.** DLPL sells to patients (B2C). Thyrocare sells to 10,800 franchisees and
   partners (B2B). Owning both means DLPL makes money from the patients who *don't* walk into a
   DLPL centre.
3. **Cost.** Thyrocare runs about 210 million tests a year through heavily automated labs. DLPL
   could route high-volume routine tests there and buy reagents for both companies at bigger scale.
4. **Growth.** Thyrocare grew revenue 21% in FY26 and 24% in Q1 FY27, against DLPL's 12% and 19%.
5. **Timing.** The shares have been de-risked: the pledge overhang is gone and 10% has been
   placed with long-term institutions.

### The case against, with evidence

1. **The seller now holds the cards.** In 2025 API had pledged shares and debt to repay, and sold
   at ~₹418 in today's shares **(derived)**. Since August 2026 it is **debt-free** and has "no
   need to sell". A buyer's best moment was 12 months ago. Any deal now needs a real premium.
2. **Channel conflict.** Thyrocare's franchisees and partner labs compete with DLPL. If their
   wholesale supplier becomes their biggest rival's subsidiary, some will leave for
   Metropolis, Redcliffe, Agilus or others. **This is the single biggest risk to Thyrocare's
   revenue**, and it doesn't show up in any spreadsheet.
3. **The PharmEasy dependency.** PharmEasy cross-sells diagnostics through its app and is both
   Thyrocare's owner and a major B2B partner. After the sale, would PharmEasy keep routing tests to
   a DLPL-owned lab? Unless a long-term supply agreement is part of the deal, some of Thyrocare's
   revenue walks out of the door with the seller. **The size of this revenue is in Thyrocare's
   related-party note, which I could not download (verify, top priority).**
4. **Brand clash.** DLPL is premium and Thyrocare is cheap. Put them together badly and DLPL's
   own patients start asking why they pay double. The two would almost certainly have to run as
   separate brands.
5. **Minority leakage.** Unless DLPL buys 100%, up to 49% of Thyrocare's profit belongs to other
   shareholders. Every transaction between DLPL and Thyrocare becomes a related-party transaction
   that minority holders will watch closely.

## Part 7: The one question to answer next

**How much of Thyrocare's revenue depends on PharmEasy, and is it under contract?**

If it is small, the deal logic above holds. If it is large and has no contract, API Holdings is
selling DLPL a company whose second-biggest customer is itself, and the price must come down.
Look for the answer in the **Thyrocare FY26 annual report (related-party note)**, and look in the
**2021 letter of offer**, which sets out what PharmEasy planned for Thyrocare when it bought in.

## Glossary

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Promoter** | The controlling owner of an Indian listed company, as declared to SEBI. |
| **SPA** | Share purchase agreement: the private contract to buy the seller's shares. |
| **SEBI SAST** | SEBI's Substantial Acquisition of Shares and Takeovers Regulations, 2011: India's takeover code. |
| **Open offer** | A legally required offer to buy shares from public shareholders when control changes. |
| **PAC** | Persons acting in concert: people acting together with the buyer. They are treated as one. |
| **VWAP** | Volume-weighted average price: the average price, weighted by how many shares traded at each price. |
| **Escrow** | Money locked in a separate account to guarantee the buyer can pay. |
| **Minimum public shareholding** | At least 25% of a listed company must be held by the public. |
| **CCI** | Competition Commission of India, which clears mergers that could hurt competition. |
| **Pledge** | Using shares as security for a loan. The lender can sell them if the loan isn't repaid. |
| **NCD** | Non-convertible debenture: a bond that can't be turned into shares. |
| **Bonus issue** | Free extra shares to existing holders. The company is worth the same; the price per share falls to match. |
| **EBITDA** | Earnings before interest, tax, depreciation and amortisation: roughly, operating cash profit. |
| **PAT** | Profit after tax: the final profit. |
| **P/E** | Price ÷ earnings: how many years of today's profit the market is paying for. |
| **Earnings yield** | The inverse of P/E: profit per ₹100 of price. |
| **EPS accretion / dilution** | Whether a deal raises or lowers the buyer's earnings per share. |
| **Preferential allotment** | Issuing new shares to a chosen party (here, API Holdings) instead of to everyone. |
